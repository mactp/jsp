Friends of Jacob Smith Park

Get involved – how you can help JSP thrive

Volunteer with us

Help us keep the park special for people, wildlife and future generations.

The Friends of Jacob Smith Park (FoJSP) are all volunteers, dedicated to maintaining and enhancing the park for everyone’s enjoyment and to support a rich, diverse variety of wildlife.

What we do

Part of our work includes regular and ongoing maintenance, such as annual oiling of the oak benches, hand-pulling balsam in early summer and general tidying in the park.

We also undertake one-off projects such as tree planting, sowing of wildflowers and the addition of new benches — see the list of recent projects at the bottom of this page.

Keeping the park beautiful takes time, so we really appreciate it when people who enjoy visiting the park can also help with maintenance jobs or one-off projects. Giving your time to volunteer with us — no matter how much or little, or whether it is regular or a one-off — is very precious and really does make a huge difference.

Regular volunteering days

First Wednesday of every month (March–October). We meet at 10:00 just inside the gate and finish around 12:00.

We undertake a variety of jobs from light tasks to more physically demanding work — you choose what to get involved in. We’d love you to join us, either regularly or as a one-off, for all or part of the session.

You can let us know you’re coming via our contact form or simply turn up at 10:00. (If you can, please bring gloves.)

To enquire about other volunteer opportunities or get involved with any of our other work, please email friends@jacobsmithpark.co.uk.

Current projects

Wildflowers project

In 2024, we began a project to increase the range of native plants in the park. The aim is to extend the period of nectar availability and provide foodplants to support and encourage butterflies, other pollinators and wildlife. The first planting focused on the perimeter of the park and a few areas where the grass is already naturally low-growing.

Autumn 2024

After preparing the ground by strimming and raking, we planted plugs and sowed seed. We expected some species to take better than others and used the outcomes to guide future planting. Key species include yellow rattle, garlic mustard (foodplant of orange tip and green-veined white butterflies), red campion, knapweed, yarrow and bird’s-foot trefoil (foodplant of the common blue butterfly). We also planted comfrey, wild carrot, willowherb (foodplant of the elephant hawk moth), red clover, vetches, selfheal, pignut (foodplant of the chimney sweeper moth), hemp agrimony, scabious, upright hedge parsley, hedge woundwort, teasel and wild angelica.

In December 2024 we also planted native shrubs — mainly species not previously planted in the park — including alder buckthorn (brimstone butterfly foodplant), dogwood, guelder rose, wild honeysuckle and spindle, chosen to provide flowers, fruit, colour and food for insects.

Summer 2025

With very little rain from February onwards, Yorkshire experienced drought conditions. In early April, when 1st Scriven cubs came to help plant spring plug plants, the ground was already hard, so we planted into molehills. Growth was delayed by the heat and dry conditions. Eventually yellow rattle germinated and grew, as did red bartsia (a bonus from previous ground disturbance). Both are parasitic on grass and will help weaken it in future. Red campion and garlic mustard also grew well. As of early July, the majority of shrubs were still alive. Other plants struggled, becoming stunted. More planting is planned for autumn and next spring, with fingers crossed for kinder weather.

Balsam management

Himalayan balsam is an invasive plant that spreads aggressively and out-competes native plants, reducing biodiversity. It had flourished in JSP and was tackled as a recent project using a contractor. It still needs ongoing control through volunteer activity — hoeing in spring and hand-pulling in summer. We aim to prevent balsam setting seed, reducing the problem year by year.

Recent projects

  • Addition of seven new oak benches.
  • Installation of 12 new bat, owl and bird boxes.
  • Three-year programme employing a contractor to reduce balsam to a manageable level.
  • New information board at the park entrance.
  • Planting of over 300 trees.
  • Planting of over 200 shrubs and plugs, and sowing of wildflowers to attract pollinators.
  • Regular summer “Talks in the Park” by local experts (bees, wildflower meadows, benefits of parks and wild spaces, astronomy, and more).
  • Renovation of ponds that had dried out.
  • Replacement of broken fences around veteran trees.
  • Installation of a boot scraper at the park entrance.
