Wildlife of Jacob Smith Park

Habitats & seasons

Stepping through the gate you’ll find a peaceful semi-wild mosaic of grassland, woodland, hedgerow and ponds—home to a wide range of wildlife.

Explore an overview of habitats, what to spot by season, and how volunteers help nature thrive here.

Overview

In its most recent history the park was grazing land, and the grasslands still dominate. Even within that there’s variety: in summer, tall, strongly-growing grasses can hide the views; in other areas, short turf reveals herbs and wildflowers. The park also includes small areas of mixed deciduous woodland, wildlife ponds, damp meadows and scrub hedging around the perimeter.

Tip: slow down and scan edges—where habitats meet is where much of the action happens.

The park through the seasons

Winter / new year

At the start of the year, even with frosty nights, some spring butterflies begin to emerge; many species lay eggs on early growth of nettles and garlic mustard. Mole activity becomes obvious as new tunnels are dug and hundreds of molehills appear. Greater spotted woodpeckers drum territorially on dead limbs high in veteran oaks. Rooks return from nearby woods to their rookery near the entrance, noisily repairing winter damage. Many birds breed in the park: old oaks provide hollows for nuthatch and tits, sparrows bustle in hedges, and a song thrush often sings along the northern boundary.

Spring

By early April swallows and house martins arrive from Africa and feed over the grasslands on abundant insects. Stand by the wildlife pond to see them swoop for mud to build nests. By the end of April, swifts join the frenzy. Late spring brings a burst of colour: red and white clover fuel bees and butterflies; buttercups turn meadows yellow; common spotted and northern marsh orchids emerge in number.

Summer

With warmth and growth, grassland butterflies appear in huge numbers, nectaring on thistles, bramble, knapweed and ragwort and laying eggs on specific grasses. Around 20 butterfly species have been recorded (almost half the UK total), making mid-July perfect for joining the Big Butterfly Count.

Dragonflies emerge too. Emperor dragonflies patrol like tiny helicopters; common blue damselflies hunt among grasses; metallic blue banded demoiselles dance by the ponds; broad-bodied chasers with powder-blue males chase over open water. Foxes are occasionally seen, and early visitors sometimes spot deer grazing.

Autumn

Ivy comes into flower and fruit ripens across the park—apples, plums, hips, haws and elderberry feed wildlife stocking up for winter. Late butterflies top up on ivy nectar and fallen fruit. Squirrels cache nuts in the woods. After the grass cut in October the long views reopen; tussocky areas shelter voles and shrews, attracting kestrel, red kite and buzzard—especially when the park is quiet.

How the FoJSP supports wildlife

  • Installed small bird, owl and bat boxes to provide additional nesting and roosting sites.
  • Great crested newt project with Yorkshire Wildlife Trust (2024: new pond dug and the existing pond cleared).
  • Planting wildflowers and flowering shrubs to extend nectar and food plants for pollinators.
  • Maintaining grasslands and raking the wildflower meadow so meadow flowers keep flourishing.
  • Planted an area of mixed trees (Nov 2023) to develop into a connecting area of woodland.
  • Fenced ponds to prevent disturbance and protect aquatic invertebrates (including from flea-treatment residues on dogs).
  • Fenced veteran trees to prevent root compaction from footfall.

Value of ancient trees

The Woodland Trust’s Ancient Tree Inventory maps the oldest and most important trees in the UK. JSP has 22 trees listed — including two classed as Ancient: the fallen oak in the centre and an ancient ash by the gate whose trunk now forms an archway.

Some trees may be 400–500 years old—possibly planted as part of a historic deer park. Ancient trees can support up to 2,300 wildlife species, providing habitat for insects, birds, mammals and fungi.

Watercourses & springs

Jacob Smith Park has three ponds—two wildlife ponds and a seasonal drainage pool. In very wet winters the ponds connect as water flows diagonally across the grass from south to north. Underground springs can sometimes be heard “hissing” in the meadow after heavy rain and may bubble up along the paths.

The damp character of parts of the park shapes what grows—look for hairy willowherb, cuckoo flower, goat willow, dogwoods and ragged robin.
